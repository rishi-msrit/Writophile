---
title: "Chapter 2: The Eaves of Marudhamalai"
---

The sound of the crash inside the glasshouse seemed to linger in the air, competing with the heavy roar of the rain outside. 

Anbarasi looked at the locked iron doors, then up at Rudra. His face had gone pale, his gaze fixed on the handle. 

"What was that?" she asked, her voice tight with concern. "Something fell."

Rudra didn't answer immediately. He reached into his pocket and pulled out a heavy, tarnished brass key. His fingers were steady, but he moved with a sudden urgency. He slotted the key into the rusted padlock, turned it with a heavy click, and pushed the doors open.

They stepped inside, leaving the cold rain behind for the warm, humid air of the greenhouse. It smelled of damp earth, rust, and old decay. 

A few feet away from the entrance, they found the source of the noise. A large pane of glass from the roof had given way under the wind, shattering onto a wooden workbench. The impact had knocked over a heavy clay pot, spilling dark soil across the floor.

Anbarasi immediately walked over, stepping carefully around the shards of glass. She looked up at the broken roof panel, where rain was now dripping through, falling directly onto a bench of delicate plants.

"The wind must have loosened it," she said, examining the bench. She pointed to a shelf of orchids just below the leak. "If they get too much rainwater directly on their crown, the roots will rot. We need to move them."

She reached for one of the pots, but Rudra stepped in front of her, blocking her path.

"Don't touch them," he said, his voice flat. "I told you to leave."

Anbarasi looked up at him, adjusting her baseball cap. "I am not leaving them to drown, Rudra. Look at this."

She reached down, pointing to the underside of a large, faded leaf on the struggling orchid pot she had just moved. Hiding in the crevice of the stem was a small, metallic blue carpenter bee, its wings tucked tightly against its body to stay dry.

"A blue carpenter bee," she said, her voice softening. "They are the main pollinators for this specific hybrid. They nest in the old wood here. The glasshouse isn't dead. The insects are still here, waiting for the rain to clear so they can do their work. If you let these plants die, the whole ecosystem of this garden goes with them."

Rudra stared at the tiny bee, then at her. The anger in his eyes seemed to fade, replaced by a quiet, brooding look. He looked at her wet clothes, the magnifying lens hanging around her neck, and the stubborn set of her jaw.

"You think you are very smart, don't you?" he asked quietly. "A college student coming here to solve everything."

"I just want to do my job," Anbarasi said, meeting his gaze. "And save something beautiful."

Rudra was silent for a long moment, the rain drumming a steady rhythm on the glass panels above them. He looked down at the brass key in his hand, then back at her.

"Three weeks," he said suddenly.

Anbarasi blinked. "What?"

"I'll give you three weeks," Rudra said, his tone turning cold again, though the hostility was gone. "If you can bring even a single hybrid orchid back to health in three weeks, you keep the key. You finish your thesis, and I won't interfere."

"And if I can't?"

"Then you pack your things, leave this estate, and never show your face here again. Deal?"

Anbarasi looked at him. She knew three weeks was a short time for sick plants, but she couldn't let this opportunity go. She nodded.

"Deal," she said, holding out her hand.

Rudra looked at her open palm. He hesitated, then placed the key in her hand. His warm, calloused palm brushed against her skin, a brief touch that sent a sudden warmth through her. He let go quickly, stepping back.

"The clock is ticking, Anbarasi," he said.

He turned and walked out of the glasshouse, heading back to the main house through the pouring rain without looking back.

Anbarasi stood alone in the quiet greenhouse. She looked at the key in her hand, then walked back to the workbench to clean up the spilled soil from the shattered clay pot.

She knelt down, scooping up the dark earth to put it back into a new pot. As her fingers cleared the roots of the broken plant, her hand brushed against something hard and smooth buried deep in the soil.

She pulled it out. 

It was a small, green glass vial, completely empty and unlabeled. She held it up to the dim light, using her magnifying lens to inspect it. 

Stamped into the glass near the base was a tiny, faded logo of the Raghavan Group. She smelled the opening of the vial; it carried a faint, bitter chemical odor that had nothing to do with fertilizer.

Anbarasi’s heart hammered against her chest. The plant hadn't died of neglect—it had been poisoned.
