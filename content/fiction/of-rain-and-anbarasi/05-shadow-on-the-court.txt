---
title: "Chapter 5: Shadow on the Court"
---

By the eighth day of the wager, the glasshouse had undergone a quiet transformation. The broken roof panel was weatherproofed, the debris-choked drainage trenches were running clear, and the heavy morning air felt alive with renewed vitality.

On the central wooden staging, three of the struggling Raghavan Crimson Orchids were showing their first fresh signs of recovery. Their swollen pseudobulbs had regained their firmness, and two slender emerald flower spikes had begun to nudge through the sheath of dried bracts. Above them, buzzing softly in the morning sunbeams, two metallic blue carpenter bees hovered over the damp moss, inspecting their restored territory.

Anbarasi was leaning over the potting bench, examining the underside of an orchid leaf through her magnifying loupe, when the iron door creaked open. 

Rudra walked in, holding a worn, cloth-bound notebook under his arm. He wore a simple navy cotton shirt with the sleeves folded to his elbows and clean dark jeans. Over the past four days, the sharp, hollow lines around his cheekbones had softened, replaced by the clean color that comes from physical labor in open air.

He stopped beside her, setting the notebook down on the teak bench. 

"My mother's personal journal," he said quietly. "I found it in the library study upstairs. She kept detailed logs of the temperature cycles and the soil blends she used when she first cross-pollinated the Ghat orchids with the Himalayan stock."

Anbarasi looked at the yellowed pages filled with elegant cursive handwriting, detailed pencil sketches of petals, and pressed dried leaves. A rush of reverence touched her. 

"She was brilliant," Anbarasi whispered, looking up at him. "This level of botanical documentation is what university professors spend decades trying to compile. Why did you never look at this before?"

Rudra leaned back against the potting table, his gaze dropping to his hands. "Because looking at it felt like a reminder of what I took from her. After the scandal broke in Chennai, she got sick so fast. Her heart couldn't take the stress. The press was camped outside our gate in Coimbatore twenty-four hours a day. They had megaphones, flashing cameras at her bedroom window whenever the curtains moved."

He paused, a dark shadow flickering across his eyes. "Everyone believed what they read on the front pages. The sports federation, the board, even my father. They called it a selfish stunt to boost my stamina for the finals."

"Who did it, Rudra?" Anbarasi asked gently.

Rudra’s jaw hardened, his hands curling into tight fists at his sides. "Karthik. We had been playing together since we were nine years old at the tennis academy in Bangalore. We shared hotel rooms, training diets, rackets. We were doubles partners. But the state association had only one wildcard sponsorship for the international satellite tour in Europe."

He let out a sharp, ragged breath. "The night before the semifinals, we were reviewing tape in the lounge. He brought me my electrolyte bottle from the trainer's cooler, like he had done a hundred times before. It was laced with a synthetic peptide—something rare that doesn't show on routine screening but trips the international Olympic panel. Within twenty-four hours of the match, the sample was flagged. Karthik got the European slot. I got an indefinite suspension."

"Enna oru kevalamana manithan (What a despicable man)," Anbarasi breathed, her dark eyes flashing with genuine anger. "To destroy your life for a ticket?"

"I trusted him," Rudra said, his voice dropping to a harsh whisper. "That was my crime. I was blind."

Before Anbarasi could speak, the sharp crunch of gravel sounded from outside the glasshouse wall. 

Through the clouded panels, the dark silhouette of a man moved quickly along the overgrown perimeter of the garden. A second later, the metallic click and whir of a motorized telephoto lens cut through the quiet air.

Snap. Click-whir.

Rudra’s entire body went rigid. The color drained from his face until his skin looked like ash. 

Outside the glass door stood a wiry man in a faded safari vest, holding a professional DSLR camera with an extended zoom lens pointed directly into the glasshouse. He was peering through the glass, his finger working the shutter in rapid bursts.

Snap. Snap. Click-whir.

"Rudra Raghavan! Exclusive pictures of the banned prodigy in hiding!" the man muttered loudly to himself, stepping closer to frame the shot through the open doorway.

The sudden intrusion triggered a visceral, paralyzing panic in Rudra. His breath caught in his throat in shallow, panicked gasps. The sound of the motorized shutter seemed to echo in his skull, dragging him straight back to the nightmare of flashing strobes, shouting reporters blocking his mother's ambulance, and the suffocating wall of lenses that had trapped him inside his own home. He stood frozen, his eyes wide and vacant, unable to move a single muscle as his hands trembled violently.

The reporter stepped boldly over the threshold, raising the camera to Rudra’s face. "Rudra-sir, just two questions! Are you planning to appeal the ban, or is the Raghavan family cutting you off completely—"

"Veliya po! (Get out!)"

The sudden shout was like a crack of thunder. 

Anbarasi did not hesitate. The gentle, soft-spoken student who normally shrank from strangers moved with explosive speed. She stepped directly into the aisle, placing her body between the camera lens and Rudra, completely blocking the reporter's line of sight.

The photographer blinked, annoyed. "Hey, move aside, girl. I'm doing my job—"

"Idhu private property! Inga oru photovum edukka koodadhu! (This is private property! You will not take a single photograph here!)" she yelled, her voice vibrating with fierce authority. Her dark eyes blazed beneath the brim of her baseball cap.

When the man attempted to raise the camera above her shoulder to capture Rudra's panicked face, Anbarasi grabbed the heavy brass high-pressure misting hose hooked to the estate cistern. With a fierce wrench of her wrist, she twisted the brass nozzle wide open.

A powerful jet of icy, mud-tinted rainwater blasted across the threshold, striking the reporter square in the chest and face. 

The man shrieked, stumbling backwards over a pile of broken clay pots. Water slammed into his camera lens, drenching his clothes and knocking his glasses askew. 

"Aiyo! Enna panra paithiyakari? (What are you doing, crazy woman?)" the reporter sputtered, choking on the spray as he scrambled to shield his soaked electronics.

"Olunga ippove odi po, illana security-a kuppiduven! (Run away right now, or I will call security!)" Anbarasi shouted, advancing on him with the brass nozzle raised like a weapon, spraying a relentless barrage at his feet. "Indha pakkam thirumbi kooda paaka koodadhu! (Don't you dare even look in this direction again!)"

Terrified by her sheer ferocity, the soaked photographer clutched his dripping camera to his chest, scrambled up the gravel path, and sprinted toward the estate gates as fast as his legs could carry him.

Anbarasi shut off the valve. The roar of the water died down, leaving only the sound of heavy drips splashing onto the stone floor. 

Her chest rose and fell in rapid breaths. Her dark cotton shirt was spattered with water, her hair was escaping her braid in unruly strands, and the skin of her face, warm and radiant like sun-baked earth after rain, was flushed with fierce adrenaline. She stood tall in the doorway, the heavy brass nozzle still gripped firmly in her hand, guarding the threshold like a warrior protecting a temple.

Behind her, the suffocating panic finally broke its hold on Rudra. 

He stared at her back in stunned, absolute silence. In twelve agonizing months of disgrace, no one had ever stepped into the storm for him. His sponsors had terminated his contracts within hours. His friends had stopped answering his calls. His father had locked him away out of sheer embarrassment. 

Yet this quiet girl from a modest nursery—a girl who had every reason to walk away from his hostility—had thrown herself directly in front of the lens to protect his dignity.

Anbarasi turned around slowly, lowering the brass hose. Seeing the lingering shock in his dark eyes and the faint tremor in his hands, her fierce expression softened into an infinite warmth.

She stepped toward him, setting the hose aside on the bench. 

"Neenga thaniya illa, Rudra (You are not alone, Rudra)," she said softly, her voice steady and tender. 

Rudra looked at her, his throat tightening with an emotion so powerful it threatened to tear him apart. He reached out slowly, his large, calloused hand trembling as his fingers brushed the damp sleeve of her shirt. His touch lingered there on her arm, warm and grounding, as the heavy silence of the glasshouse wrapped around them like a sanctuary against the world.
