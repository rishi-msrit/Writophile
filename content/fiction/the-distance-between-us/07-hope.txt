title: "Chapter Seven: Hope"
date: "2025-04-22"

The night had long since settled, quiet and dense like a secret. The streetlights
outside cast a soft glow through Rebecca's window, painting golden stripes across
her bedroom floor. She sat curled at the edge of her bed, knees drawn to her
chest, hoodie sleeves pulled over her hands like armor. The warmth of his arms
still lingered, a ghost against her skin.
She'd been staring at the wall for the better part of an hour. Not thinking—just...
feeling. And that was the problem.
She was feeling too much.
Everything about earlier kept replaying in loops. His voice, his touch, the way he
didn't flinch when she broke. The way he held her like it meant something. Like
she meant something.
Her phone buzzed once. A message from him.
"Home safe. I meant what I said."
She didn't reply. Not because she didn't want to. But because her fingers trembled
at the idea of saying anything at all. She didn't trust her words tonight. They'd
either crumble or explode.
Her throat ached, the kind of dull ache that came after crying too hard and too
long. And her chest felt hollow. Not in the numb way she was used to, but in a
way that made space for something else. Something terrifying.
Hope.
Rebecca hated it. Hated how much she wanted to believe him. Hated how warm
his voice made her feel. Hated how her mind kept dragging her back to the way
his hand fit at the small of her back like it belonged there.
She closed her eyes.
And she was six again.
It was a rainy afternoon. The sky was the color of lead, thunder growling
somewhere distant. Her mother had just found the crumpled math test at the
bottom of her bag. Forty-two out of a hundred. Big red circles all over it.


"I work so hard to give you a life, and this is what you do?" her mother had
shouted, voice shrill, echoing against the tiled kitchen walls. "You're just like him.
Useless. Always disappointing."
She remembered standing there, her little pink raincoat still on, the zipper stuck
at the top. Her shoes squeaked on the floor, and her tiny fingers clutched the test
paper like it might bite her. The tears came quietly. She'd learned early not to cry
too loud.
That night, she waited at the window for her dad. He'd promised—promised—he'd
come by and take her for ice cream after school. Strawberry with sprinkles. Her
favorite.
He never showed up.
She waited until the sky turned purple and her eyes burned from trying not to
blink. That was the first time she said it to herself.
Don't depend on anyone. Don't trust promises. Don't hope.
And now, all these years later, one boy with soft eyes and a gentle voice was
undoing everything she'd built. She hated how much she wanted to let him.
Her phone buzzed again.
"Don't expect me to give up on you; I'll wait however long it takes."
Her chest tightened, and a small, involuntary noise escaped her throat. She buried
her face in the pillow.
Why did he have to say things like that?
Why did he have to mean them?
The next evening, she saw him. He was sitting on the stone wall outside the
library, earbuds in, one leg propped up as he scrolled through his phone. The sun
hit his hair just right.
He looked up, like he'd sensed her. He always did.
Their eyes met. He didn't smile—not right away. He just looked at her, like he
was checking to see if she was okay. Like he was trying to read all the things she
wouldn't say out loud.
She took a shaky step forward. Then another.
When she reached him, he pulled one earbud out and tilted his head slightly.


"Hey."
"Hey," she echoed, her voice softer than she meant it to be.
There was a pause. A moment too long. And then she sat beside him, not looking
at him, but close enough that their shoulders almost brushed.
He didn't push. Didn't ask. Just waited.
And somehow, that made her want to talk.
"I didn't sleep much," she said after a beat.
"Me neither."
"Too much on your mind?"
"Yeah," he said, then added, "Mostly you."
She laughed softly, more out of disbelief than anything else. "You're impossible."
"I know," he said, and she could hear the smile in his voice. "But you're still here."
She didn't answer. But she didn't move away either.
That silence between them—it wasn't awkward. It was something else. Something
that felt like a breath held too long. Like a secret on the edge of spilling.
And when he finally turned to look at her, he reached up and gently tucked a
strand of hair behind her ear.
And God, the way her stomach twisted at that—at such a small, stupidly intimate
thing—it made her feel like she was falling.
Falling in ways she promised herself she never would.
He asked her if she wanted to get something to eat. She said yes before her brain
could talk her out of it.
They ended up sitting at an outdoor table. Rebecca chewed on a fry, eyes
narrowing at the ice cream in front of her. "You weren't kidding. This looks like
regret."
He laughed, leaning back in his seat. "It tastes like one too."
She took a tiny scoop, then made a face. "This is literally cold toothpaste."
He grinned. "Still, you keep eating it."


"I hate wasting things."
"Is that what this is?" he asked, voice low but laced with something playful.
"Wasting time with me?"
She rolled her eyes, but her lips curved. "I don't know. You're tolerable, I guess."
"Tolerable. Wow. That's practically a love confession."
They both smiled, and for the first time in days, it wasn't laced with sadness. It
was just... light.
Simple.
The way teenage things should be.
Their legs accidentally touched under the table, and she didn't move away.
Neither did he.
He looked at her then—really looked. "You seem a little better today."
She shrugged, biting her lower lip. "I am. I think."
He leaned forward, elbows on the table. "What's on your mind?"
She hesitated. Then said, "You."
It slipped out before she could stop it.
She didn't know why she said it. Maybe because his eyes were too gentle, or
maybe because being near him was starting to feel less like a risk and more like a
relief.
He blinked. Slowly.
And then, like gravity was optional, the space between them thinned.
She could feel it—his presence, his heat, the quiet tension stretching thinner and
thinner between them like a string about to snap.
He didn't touch her. Not yet. But his fingers drummed lightly on the table,
inching closer to hers with every beat.
"I think about you too," he said softly.
She didn't know what to do with that. So she just stared at his hand. Watched it
like it was a flame and she was scared of getting burned.


"I think about what you're feeling," he continued. "What you're hiding. What
you're scared of."
She looked up.
His voice was barely above a whisper. "And I keep wishing I could take some of it
away."
Rebecca blinked fast.
God, she hated how easy he made it sound. Like love wasn't a battlefield but a
blanket. Something you could wrap around someone. Something that didn't
demand anything back.
He moved his hand again. This time, it grazed hers—just a soft brush, like he was
asking permission in silence.
She didn't flinch.
Her fingers opened slightly. Almost like an accident. Like her body had already
decided before her mind could interfere.
They walked back slowly, not in a rush to return to the noise of reality. Every
now and then, their hands would brush again. Sometimes they'd stay touching.
Other times they'd drift apart, like they were shy about holding on too long,
stopped walking near an empty park bench. 
"I had a recurring dream when I was a kid," she said suddenly. "I was underwater.
Drowning. But there were these hands—always these same hands—that would
pull me out. Not my mom's. Not my dad's. Just... someone."
He looked at her. Quiet.
"And sometimes I wonder if that dream was about you. Before I even knew you
existed."
His chest rose slowly.
And then he leaned just a little closer.
Not enough to scare her.
But enough for her to feel it—the possibility.
"I'm not going anywhere, Rebecca," he said. "Even if you push. Even if you break."
She turned her face to his. Their noses were almost touching now. Her breath


hitched again.
The air was electric. Heavy. Soft. Dangerous.
And when he reached up—slowly, so slowly—and ran his thumb across her
cheek, she closed her eyes for half a second and leaned into it.
There was no kiss.
Not yet.
But there didn't need to be.
Because everything in that moment felt like one.
His hand was still close. Not gripping hers, just... there. Like a promise waiting to
be kept.
She swallowed, her voice barely above a murmur. "Sometimes I don't think I
know how to be loved."
She hesitated for a while, then said it"Because most of the love I've known came
with a price tag. My dad? He loved me when I got good grades. My mom? Only
when I smiled enough to make her look like a good parent."
His eyes didn't blink. Didn't pity. Just listened.
"That's the thing," she continued, her voice tight. "Everyone wanted a version of
me. The quiet one. The perfect one. The girl who didn't cry too loud, didn't ask
for too much, didn't make a mess."
Her lip trembled. "So I became her. And I hated her."
He leaned forward, barely an inch between them now. "I like the messy one
better."
Rebecca let out a shaky breath, and then—impulsively, without thinking—she
rested her forehead against his.
"You're not broken," he whispered. "You're just learning to feel again."
"I don't want to scare you," she said, voice cracking.
He pulled back slightly, just enough to look her in the eyes. "You couldn't. Not
even if you tried."
Later that night, back in her room, she sat with the lights oﬀ and her knees pulled
to her chest, trying to hold onto the warmth he'd left behind. Like she could


bottle it. Save it for the nights when the shadows came back louder than her
heartbeat.
Her phone buzzed once.
him: you okay?
She stared at the message for a second. Then replied.
her: i don't know. but i'm better when i'm with you.
A few seconds passed.
him: then stay close.
She didn't sleep much that night.
Not because of nightmares.
But because her mind kept replaying the way he looked at her—like she was
something soft in a world made of sharp things.
