The next afternoon, she found him waiting under a tree near the school's back
gate.
He was leaning against the trunk like he belonged there, earbuds in, one hand
tucked into his jacket pocket. When he saw her, he smiled—lazy, warm, the kind
of smile that refused to let go.
She walked up to him, arms folded, eyebrows raised. "Stalking me now?"
"Maybe." He took out one earbud. "Or maybe I just knew you'd be here."
She scoﬀed playfully. "So full of yourself."
"I'm full of fries. I brought extra."
He held up a paper bag like a peace oﬀering.
She took it, but didn't hide the grin tugging at her lips.
They sat on the grass, legs tangled like they were tired of pretending space
mattered. He watched her eat a fry with far too much interest.
"What?" she asked, mouth full.
"You do that thing," he said. "You chew like you're trying not to make noise."
She blinked. "I didn't even notice."
"That's the thing," he murmured. "You've been small for so long, you don't realize
you don't have to be."
Rebecca looked down.
He nudged her knee with his. "Take up space. Eat loud. Be loud. Cry loud. You're
allowed."
Her eyes welled up again. Stupidly. Quietly.
And then, in the most un-Rebecca-like move possible, she leaned in and kissed his
cheek.
Quick.


Barely there.
But when she pulled back, his eyes were wide and his lips parted—like she'd
knocked the air out of him.
"That's for the fries," she said.
He grinned. "You're terrible at pretending."
"I know."
And before she could change her mind, she leaned in again.
This time, it wasn't his cheek.
It was his lips.
Soft, Slow,
A question and an answer all wrapped into one.
He didn't rush. Didn't grab or push. Just held her face gently, like he was afraid
she might shatter if he held too tight.
And somewhere between the kiss and the way his thumb stroked her jaw, she
realized she wasn't scared anymore.
Not of him. Not even of herself.
They planned to walk till her place after the class after which he could return but
the sky decided to pour without warning, and his place was too far.
"I swear the rain is conspiring," she mumbled, flicking water oﬀ her sleeves as
they ran towards a shade, dripping.
"I don't mind. I like it here," he said, setting his bag down.
She looked at him over her shoulder. "In my room?"
"In your space," he corrected with a grin.
Rebecca rolled her eyes, but her lips twitched.
Ten minutes later, they were sprawled on her bed—barefoot, laughing about
something she'd said, hair still damp and her shirt sticking slightly to her back.
Her knee brushed his, and neither of them moved away.
"You always this funny?" he asked, propping his head up with his arm, his elbow


pressing into the pillow.
"You just bring it out of me," she replied, smug.
"You know," he said, watching her intently, "this version of you—the one that
giggles and makes fun of me? She's hot."
She gasped dramatically. "So the crying, emotionally scarred girl from yesterday
wasn't hot?"
"God, no. She was beautiful. But this one's dangerous."
"Dangerous how?" she asked, voice dipping slightly, teasing.
He smirked. "She makes me want to do really stupid things."
Her breath caught, just for a moment.
Then she leaned closer, hand resting lightly on his stomach over his T-shirt. "Like
what?"
He didn't answer right away. Sat up slightly, cupping her cheek, his thumb
brushing her lower lip like he was testing a theory.
She didn't stop him.
Didn't move.
She only whispered, "You always this gentle?"
"Only with things I don't wanna break.
His lips met hers again—this time slower, deeper, like they had nowhere else to
be, like the world outside the room didn't matter anymore.
His hand found the curve of her waist, fingers curling into the fabric of her shirt,
and she shifted closer, one leg sliding partially over his.
Her breath hitched when he kissed along her jaw, lingering near the spot below
her ear that made her stomach twist in ways she didn't know were possible.
"I can hear your heartbeat," he murmured.
"Is it loud?"
"It's... fast," he smiled, lips ghosting over her skin. "Like it's running toward
something."


She smiled back, eyes half-lidded. "Maybe it's running from your cheesy lines."
He laughed into her neck. "You wound me."
Rebecca giggled, fingers moving into his hair without thinking. She felt him
breathe diﬀerently when she did that—like the contact hit deeper than expected.
And when he pulled back to look at her, his expression was something she
couldn't name.
She knew that look.
Want.
But not the kind she was used to seeing from strangers. Not the type that took.
His was slower. Warmer. It asked.
Her thumb brushed his cheekbone.
He closed his eyes like it was the softest thing he'd ever felt.
"Can I stay?" he asked quietly, eyes still shut.
"You're already here," she said.
"No," he murmured, now opening his eyes again. "I mean... tonight."
Her heart paused.
She wasn't ready for that.
But something about the way he said it—the tone, not just the words—told her it
wasn't what most people meant when they said it. There was no pressure. No
expectation.
He just didn't want to leave her alone.
And she didn't want him to go.
So she nodded.
But added, "Only if you sleep on the floor."
He gave her a mock-oﬀended look. "You're kicking me oﬀ your bed after kissing
me like that?"
"I'm preserving your dignity," she smirked. "This is a respectable establishment."


"You're evil," he muttered, reaching for a pillow.
She grinned and tossed him an extra blanket. "Goodnight, floor-boy."
But that night, even with the gap between them, they slept with their hands
stretched out, pinkies barely touching over the side of the bed.
And somehow, that felt more intimate than anything else they'd done.
Rebecca wasn't used to waking up and feeling safe. Not in her house. Not in her
bed.
But that morning, with the faint sound of rain still kissing the windows and a
quiet, steady breathing from the floor beside her, something in her chest felt
okay.
She rolled over lazily, her hair a mess, mascara smudged beneath her eyes, and
peeked down at the edge of the bed.
He was still there.
One arm thrown over his head, the other loosely holding onto the corner of the
blanket. His lips slightly parted. Hair sticking out in four diﬀerent directions.
He looked stupid. She smiled.
He was supposed to leave last night. But rain poured down so hard it drowned
the whole neighborhood in silence and flood warnings. Her parents, who had
planned an overnight trip to visit her aunt two towns away, called and told her to
stay in and not "do anything stupid." She didn't mention she already had a boy
asleep on the floor next to her bed.
And since it was Saturday, no school, no early alarms, no fake smiles for teachers
—they had all the time in the world.
And then, in a moment of quiet rebellion against the world that had hurt her so
much—she let herself feel it.
That warm, fluttery, terrifying thing that settled somewhere between her ribs.
He stirred, cracking one eye open.
"You're staring."
"I'm judging," she corrected.
"Judging what?"


"Your face." She grinned. "You snore."
"I do not—"
"You do," she cut in. "It's soft, like a dying kitten, but it's there."
He pulled the blanket over his head dramatically. "I feel attacked."
She laughed—loud, genuine. The kind of laugh that cracked something open
inside her. She wasn't used to starting mornings like this. Not with laughter. Not
with someone else.
And definitely not with someone who made her heart skip three damn beats when
he finally got up, shirt ruﬄed, rubbing his eyes like a five-year-old.
"You look like a raccoon," he told her, pointing to the smudge under her eye.
"You look like regret," she replied, flicking a pillow at him.
He caught it, stepped forward, and tossed it back—lighter this time, landing it
near her stomach. "You've got evil in you."
"You've got a death wish," she smirked.
They paused—eyes locking. Both smiling.
Then he slowly leaned over the edge of the bed, elbows pressing into the
mattress. "Rebecca?"
"Hmm?"
His hand reached out and gently tucked a loose strand of hair behind her ear.
"I really, really like you."
She blinked.
That stupid flutter in her chest returned. And panic. And something else she
couldn't name.
Her throat went dry.
"I don't know what to do with that," she whispered honestly.
"Then don't do anything." He gave her a tiny smile. "Just... exist near me."
She stared at him, something tightening behind her ribs.


Later that afternoon, after hours of lazy conversations and silent eye contact that
said way too much, they found an old box of art supplies in her room—some
dried-up acrylics, brushes, and a plain oversized white t-shirt she'd never worn.
"We're painting this?" he asked, holding it up.
"You're painting it. I'm judging you while you ruin it," Rebecca said, tossing him a
brush.
"Oh? Confident words for someone who once mistook nail polish for acrylic."
"That was one time you painted a frog pink—"
"Paint me," she said softly.
He blinked. "What?"
"You wanted a canvas. Here I am."
